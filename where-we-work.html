Where We Work

Multiple countries, one principle: we know what is actually buildable and operable on the ground before you commit.

Asia is not one market. Land tenure, labour law, permitting timelines, flood risk, truck-ban hours, working styles, and customs procedures – these differ by country and sometimes by state.

We have operated across the region since 1999. We factor the local realities into every recommendation, whether it is a warehouse site selection, a 3PL contract or a network design. The sections below are a starting point — talk to us for detail on your specific situation.

Malaysia

Kuala Lumpur, Johor, Penang, Klang Valley

Our home market and deepest expertise. The Klang Valley dominates for consumer goods distribution, while Johor serves as the gateway to Singapore and southern Thailand. Land tenure varies by state — Negeri Sembilan and Johor have different rules on foreign ownership and lease structure. We read each site against the state-level regulations that apply.

Example considerations for Malaysia

  • Land tenure rules vary by state — foreign ownership restrictions apply differently in Johor vs Selangor
  • Klang Valley labour market is tight; automation payback periods are shorter here
  • Port Klang congestion affects inbound lead times — plan buffer stock accordingly
  • Iskandar Malaysia (Johor) offers tax incentives for logistics investments

Singapore

Jurong, Changi, Tuas

The regional hub for high-value goods and export. Land scarcity means every square metre must earn its keep. Multi-storey warehouses are common and automation is essential. Labour costs are the highest in the region, making productivity improvement the primary lever.

Example considerations for Singapore

  • Multi-storey warehousing is standard — design for vertical efficiency
  • Foreign worker quota rules affect staffing plans
  • Tuas Port development will shift distribution patterns westward
  • JTC land availability is competitive — apply early in planning

Thailand

Bangkok, Laem Chabang, Chiang Mai

Bangkok dominates consumption but the city’s congestion makes last-mile delivery expensive. The Eastern Economic Corridor (Laem Chabang, Chonburi, Rayong) is the growth zone for manufacturing logistics.

Example considerations for Thailand

  • Bangkok truck ban hours (06:00-09:00, 16:00-20:00) constrain delivery windows
  • Flood risk in Bangkok — minimum floor levels and contingency routing essential
  • Eastern Economic Corridor incentives for logistics and distribution investments
  • Labour costs rising 5-8% annually — factor into long-term models

Vietnam

Ho Chi Minh City, Hanoi, Da Nang

The fastest-growing market in Southeast Asia. Southern Vietnam (HCMC and surrounding provinces) is the manufacturing heartland. Northern Vietnam serves China+1 strategies. Infrastructure is developing rapidly but road quality and port capacity can be constraints.

Example considerations for Vietnam

  • Tet holiday (late January/early February) shuts operations for 1-2 weeks — plan inventory cover
  • Industrial land prices in HCMC have doubled in 5 years
  • Customs procedures are improving but still slower than Singapore or Malaysia
  • Labour availability is abundant but turnover is high — factor into training costs

Indonesia

Jakarta, Surabaya, Bekasi, Cikarang

The largest market in Southeast Asia by population, and the most complex. Java dominates economic activity but intra-island logistics are challenging. The new capital (Nusantara) in East Kalimantan may shift distribution patterns over the next decade.

Example considerations for Indonesia

  • Jakarta traffic is among the worst in the world — last-mile delivery windows are tight
  • Foreign investment rules continue to evolve, with certain logistics activities subject to ownership, licensing, or partnership requirement
  • Religious holidays (Lebaran) affect operations — plan for extended closures
  • Cikarang and Bekasi are the main logistics corridors east of Jakarta

Philippines

Manila, Cebu, Clark

An archipelago of 7,000 islands creates unique logistics challenges. Luzon (Manila and Clark) is the main distribution hub. Visayas (Cebu) serves the central islands. Mindanao is emerging for agri-logistics. Port congestion in Manila is a persistent issue.

Example considerations for Philippines

  • Inter-island shipping adds 3-7 days to lead times — plan inventory positioning carefully
  • Manila port congestion — consider Batangas or Subic as alternatives
  • Clark Freeport Zone offers tax holidays and modern warehouse stock
  • E-commerce growth is driving demand for urban fulfilment centres

India

Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai

A continent-sized market where supply chains are often regional rather than national. Delhi NCR, Mumbai, Bengaluru and Chennai each function as major logistics hubs with their own catchment areas. Infrastructure has improved significantly in recent years, but long distances, congestion and state-by-state operating differences still shape distribution network design.

Example considerations for India

  • Interstate movements are smoother than a decade ago, but state-level regulations and compliance requirements still matter
  • Congestion around major cities can add significant time and cost to last-mile operations
  • Industrial and logistics parks are expanding rapidly along key freight corridors
  • Labour is widely available, but productivity and retention vary considerably by location

Taiwan

Taipei, Taoyuan

A compact, highly industrialised market with excellent infrastructure and short delivery distances. Most consumption is concentrated in the north around Taipei, while manufacturing activity is centred around Taoyuan, Hsinchu and Taichung. The market is operationally straightforward, although industrial land can be difficult and expensive to secure.

Example considerations for Taiwan

  • Industrial land and modern warehouse space are limited near major manufacturing clusters
  • Labour costs are higher than in most Asian markets
  • Ports, airports and road networks are highly reliable, supporting fast domestic distribution
  • Geopolitical risk should be considered when planning long-term supply chains

China

Still the manufacturing centre of Asia despite the growth of China+1 strategies. The Yangtze River Delta and Greater Bay Area remain the country's most important logistics and industrial regions, while inland cities continue to attract investment as costs rise on the coast. Infrastructure is among the best in the world, but market size and regional differences make network planning critical.

Example considerations for China

  • Labour, land and operating costs vary significantly between coastal and inland markets
  • Regulatory requirements can differ by province and municipality
  • Rail, road and port infrastructure support highly efficient domestic distribution networks
  • Many manufacturers are diversifying production, but China remains central to regional supply chains

Cross-border corridors

The main cross-border lanes — Singapore-Malaysia, Malaysia-Thailand, and the emerging Vietnam-Laos-Thailand corridor — each carry their own customs procedures, duty structures and transit time profiles.

We model cross-border cost as part of network design, not as an afterthought. For clients operating across multiple markets, the border is often the biggest cost driver and the biggest service risk.
